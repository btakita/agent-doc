**The clause.** protocol.md § NodeId / PeerId now carries a decoder obligation beside the producer bound: *a decoder that cannot represent a received `NodeId`/`PeerId` exactly MUST reject the frame; it MUST NOT round, truncate, saturate, or wrap.* The producer bound was also reworded as you asked — it names the constraint (a runtime whose integers are IEEE-754 doubles, which is every JavaScript target *including* Dart, Kotlin, and TypeScript compiled to JS) instead of the source language. I also fixed a contradiction the audit surfaced: `schemas/defs.json` capped `PeerId` at 2^53−1, which made a legal `u64` frame schema-*invalid* and pushed a runtime-capability question into wire validity. And the interesting result is not the table but *how* the seven unaudited bindings got there: only js and dart carry the rule deliberately. The other seven inherit it from a parser that happens to fail closed, which is a property nothing held in place — and two lazily-cpp decoders did not hold it:

- `MsgUnpacker::read_i64` cast a msgpack `uint 64` straight to `int64_t`. `u64::MAX` decoded as `-1` — a well-formed identifier for a different node, no error anywhere. - `json_parse` *did* refuse an over-range integer, but by letting `std::stoll` throw `std::out_of_range` — a `std::logic_error`, not the `std::runtime_error` every other parser failure raises and every caller guards a decode with. The exception escaped the decode boundary entirely.

**The fixture.** `conformance/codec/nodeid_exact_range.json` — 2^53−1, 2^53+1, and `u64::MAX`, in both codecs, generated by `scripts/gen_nodeid_exact_range_fixture.py`.

Two design points worth recording, because both are traps:

1. **The fixture cannot carry its own expectation as a JSON number.** The fixture is JSON. A bare `9007199254740993` in it gets rounded by the parser *loading the file* on any double-backed runtime — so the runner would compare a rounded expectation against a rounded decode and pass against precisely the decoder it exists to catch. Wire frames are therefore raw text (json) and hex (msgpack), and the expectation is a decimal *string*. A spec-side guard walks the parsed fixture and fails on any integer above 2^53−1 appearing anywhere as a number, so the rule can't rot.
2. **The outcome can't be one boolean**, because the correct answer differs per binding. Scenarios are `exact` or `exact_or_reject`. But `exact_or_reject` alone is satisfied by a runner that decodes nothing and calls everything refused — so the 2^53−1 scenarios are `exact` controls every binding must decode, and each runner pins its accept/refuse **counts exactly** rather than "at least one". A guard that stops refusing and a decoder that stops decoding are each a failure.

**Nine runners, not a deferral.** Dropping the fixture into the corpus immediately failed all nine `check-conformance-coverage.sh` guards — they read the canonical corpus directly, so "land the spec now, adopt later" was never available.
